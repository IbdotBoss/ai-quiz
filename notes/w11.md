# Natural language processing

## What NLP is
**What it is.** Natural language processing, or NLP, is a branch of machine learning and AI. It focuses on deriving meaning from human language and handling it automatically. It covers both speech and text.

**Uses.** Chatbots, sentiment analysis of customer reviews or social media, fake news detection, translation, question answering, summarising and speech recognition. Broader application areas are translation, summarisation, text classification, information retrieval and extraction, and language generation.

**Two directions.** Natural language understanding takes a sentence and works out what it means. Natural language generation takes a formal logic statement and expresses it in natural language.

**Running example.** The lecture builds a deep model that separates spam text messages from legitimate ones, called ham.

## Text preparation
**What it is.** Cleaning raw text so it is better for analysis.

**Removing irrelevant characters.** Punctuation, special symbols, hashtags, HTML tags and encoding symbols such as \n add no meaning. After cleaning, `<p>Hey there!!!</p> #Excited` becomes Hey there Excited.

**Case normalisation.** Casing tells the model little about sentiment, so all text is lowercased. SIX chances to win CASH! becomes six chances to win cash!. This reduces irrelevant variety in the data.

**Removing outliers.** Outliers are unusual or extreme words, phrases or documents that do not fit the usual distribution. One approach drops the bottom 20% of words.

**Tokenization.** Splitting sentences into tokens. A token is usually a word but can be a subword or a character, depending on the granularity needed. It turns raw text into a form a model can process. N-grams are sequences of N tokens in a row. They capture context and which words occur together.

**Stop words.** Common words such as and, to and the carry little meaning and do not contribute to sentiment. They are filtered out before the text reaches the model.

## Stemming and lemmatization
**Stemming.** Reduces a word to its root, the stem, by removing prefixes and suffixes. Walking, walker and walks all become walk. Happily, happiness and happening all become happi, which is not a real word.

**Lemmatization.** Turns a word into its dictionary form, the lemma, using context and meaning. Went becomes go, better becomes good and cats becomes cat.

**Common mistake.** Thinking they are the same. Stemming uses rules of thumb and can give non-words. Lemmatization keeps the meaning and always gives a valid word.

| | Stemming | Lemmatization |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Cuts prefixes and suffixes by rule | Uses context and meaning |
| Output | Can be a non-word, for example happi | Always a dictionary word, for example go |

## Vectorization and TF-IDF
**What it is.** Turning text into numerical vectors that a model can take as input, since maths is easier on numbers. Techniques include bag of words, TF-IDF and one-hot encoding.

**TF-IDF.** Term frequency, inverse document frequency. It shows how important a word is to one document within a collection, the corpus. Textbooks add that a word common in one document but rare across the corpus scores high.

**Worked example.** The corpus has 200 documents and 8 of them contain aid. The document aid people health driv has 4 terms, one of which is aid.
- TF = 1 / 4 = 0.25
- IDF = ln(200 / 8) = 3.21
- TF-IDF = 0.25 x 3.21 = about 0.80

Each document is then a vector of these numbers, and the model learns to label each vector as spam or not spam.

## Embeddings
**What it is.** A special type of vectorisation. Each word becomes a dense vector of continuous values. Words used in similar contexts get similar vectors, so the vectors capture meaning. Pre-trained embeddings such as Word2Vec, GloVe and FastText are learned from very large corpora.

**Example.** King [0.4, 0.7, -0.1] and queen [0.5, 0.8, -0.2] are close. Man [0.2, 0.3, 0.6] and woman [0.3, 0.4, 0.7] are close to each other too.

**Confused with.** TF-IDF. Embeddings are dense and capture meaning. TF-IDF vectors are sparse and based on word counts.

**Embedding layer.** One-hot encoding gives long sparse vectors, which are inefficient. An embedding layer inside the network turns each word into a dense, low-dimensional vector. This captures meaning and cuts computation.

## Word order and RNNs
**The problem.** A model built on word frequencies ignores word order, but order changes meaning. Context matters too. These shoes are pretty ugly and that car is sick are hard to label, because pretty and sick can each be read two ways.

**The fix.** RNNs, especially LSTMs and GRUs from Week 10, process sequential data and keep context. The text is first turned into sequences of numbers, for example with `texts_to_sequences`, so the word order is kept.

**Padding.** The texts have different lengths, so their sequences do too. RNNs and LSTMs need input of a fixed size. `pad_sequences` pads or truncates every sequence to the same length.

**Common mistake.** Thinking padding and the embedding layer do the same job. Padding fixes the length of each sequence. The embedding layer turns each word into a dense vector.
