# Genetic algorithms

## What a genetic algorithm is
A **genetic algorithm** solves optimisation problems by imitating **natural selection**, where the fittest survive. John Holland developed it in the 1970s. It keeps a **population** of candidate solutions and improves the whole population over many **generations**.

The terms come from biology. A **chromosome** is one candidate solution, often written as a bitstring such as 101110. A **gene** is one unit of that string, such as one bit. **Fitness** measures how good a solution is and decides which are kept.

The loop runs as follows.

1. Create a random initial population.
2. Test each solution for fitness and rank them.
3. Remove some bad solutions and copy some good ones.
4. Make new candidates from the good ones with crossover and mutation.
5. Repeat from step 2 until the best solution is good enough.

> The whole bitstring is the chromosome. One bit is a gene.

## Crossover and mutation
**Crossover** joins parts of two parent chromosomes to make a child, so it reuses good features already in the population. **Mutation** makes a small random change to one chromosome, such as flipping a bit, so it brings in variety no current solution has. Crossover alone can only reshuffle what exists. Mutation lets the search reach new areas. Together they keep what works and still explore.

## Why use one
Differentiating an equation to find a minimum fails when the problem has no equation, has several minima, or has many variables and constraints. Blind generate and test ignores earlier guesses and may take too long. A genetic algorithm also generates and tests, but each generation builds on the best of the last one, and it needs no equation. It suits large search spaces such as scheduling and route planning and usually finds a good approximate answer. Its main drawback is that it can be hard to encode a problem as chromosomes with a fitness measure.

> Crossover needs two parents. Mutation changes one chromosome.
