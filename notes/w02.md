# Classification, k-NN and decision trees

## Supervised learning and classification
In **supervised learning** a model learns from labelled historical data to predict new cases. Each example has input **features** and an output **label**. **Classification** predicts a category, such as whether a loan applicant will default, yes or no. **Regression** predicts a continuous number, such as a price. The model outputs a probability for each class, and a **threshold** turns that into a label. Raising the threshold gives fewer false alarms but more missed cases.

> The type of answer decides it. A category is classification and a number on a scale is regression.

## Evaluating a classifier
A **confusion matrix** counts true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives, and every measure comes from these four. **Accuracy** is the share of all predictions that are right, and it misleads on imbalanced data. A model that always predicts the majority class can score 99% and catch nothing.

**Precision** asks, of the cases predicted positive, how many really are positive. It matters when false alarms are costly, such as a fraud team with few investigators. **Recall** asks, of all the real positives, how many were caught. It matters when a miss is costly, such as disease screening. The two pull against each other. The **F1 score** is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, so it gives one balanced number when classes are imbalanced.

> Precision looks at the predictions. Recall looks at the real cases.

## k-nearest neighbours
**k-NN** classifies a new point by measuring its distance to the training points, usually **Euclidean distance**, finding the k nearest, and taking a **majority vote** among them. For regression it averages their values.

The choice of k matters. A small k such as 1 follows single points closely, so noise can decide the class and the model **overfits** with high variance. A larger k gives smoother boundaries and resists noise, but too large a k **underfits** with high bias. The best k is chosen by testing several values on validation data with **cross-validation**.

k-NN is **lazy** because it builds no model during training. It keeps the training data and does its work at prediction time. It is **non-parametric** because it assumes no fixed form for the data. It is slow on large data, hurt by irrelevant features, and needs scaled features, otherwise the feature with the largest range dominates the distance.

> Choosing k on the training data always favours k = 1, which just memorises the training set.

## Decision trees
A **decision tree** splits the data on one feature at a time, building readable if-then rules that end in leaves. To choose a split, it tries each feature and measures how mixed the child nodes are with **Gini impurity**. Zero impurity means a node holds one class only. It keeps the split with the biggest drop in impurity, then repeats on each child. It stops when nodes are pure, no features remain, or a limit such as maximum depth is reached. A new case follows the rules down to a leaf, which predicts its majority class.

> A tree does its work in training and prediction is fast. k-NN does the opposite.
